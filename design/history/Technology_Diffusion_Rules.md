# Isekai 825: Technology Diffusion Rules

Oct 23, 2026 · @Neil Jensen

## Purpose

The player brings knowledge from the future. This doc tells the LLM how to judge **how long that knowledge takes to escape his lands** when he does nothing to spread it, and what changes that.

The question is never "how advanced is this?" It is three questions:

1. **Legibility.** Can an observer see how it works?
2. **Reproduction cost.** What does copying it actually take?
3. **Proof needed.** How long before people believe it is worth adopting?

Multiply those out and the answer falls out without a lookup table. The classic trap is assuming sophistication predicts spread: a counterweight trebuchet is far more advanced than crop rotation and spreads twenty times faster, because it is legible, cheap to copy and proves itself in an afternoon.

### Default timings (legible technologies)

| How it got out | Time to general use |
| --- | --- |
| The player gave it to an ally, taught it, or sold it openly | **1 year** |
| The player used it where others could see (a battle with allies present, a public work, a market) | **5 years** |
| Used only on his own lands, no outsiders involved | Set by the three factors below; often never |

**Viking-only engagements do not count as exposure.** A raiding band that saw a weapon and was destroyed by it takes the knowledge nowhere.

These defaults apply to things an observer can understand on sight. For anything opaque, see the factors.

## The three factors

### 1. Legibility

Can someone watching work out how it works?

| Level | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Obvious** | One look and a competent craftsman understands the principle | Counterweight trebuchet, stirrups, horse collar, wheelbarrow, padded armour, a better plough, chimneys |
| **Visible but puzzling** | The practice is plain to see, but why it works is not | Crop rotation, selective breeding, boiling water, quarantine, hygiene in a sickroom, drainage |
| **Opaque** | Watching the result teaches nothing about the method | Gunpowder, distillation, soap-making at scale, anything chemical |

**Opaque does not mean invisible.** The *process* often is. Nitre beds cannot be hidden: men collecting urine and dung, layering with ash and straw in long covered beds, turning them for a year. Everyone on the estate knows what the lord is doing, even if nobody knows why. Judge the process separately from the product.

### 2. Reproduction cost

What does copying it take?

| Level | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Trivial** | Tools and skills already present in any village | Rotation, wheelbarrow, better harness, composting |
| **Craftsman** | Needs a specific trade, available in a town | Trebuchet (carpenters), crossbows (bowyers and smiths), a watermill (millwright) |
| **Capital** | Needs sustained money and a lord's authority | Large mills, canals, major drainage, a blast furnace |
| **Programme** | A chain of processes nobody knows how to begin | Gunpowder, metallurgy beyond the period, glassmaking at scale, printing |

### 3. Proof needed

How long before people believe it is worth the change?

| Level | Meaning | Time to conviction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Self-proving** | The result is undeniable and immediate | One use |
| **A season** | Needs one cycle to show its worth | 1 year |
| **Several cycles** | Needs repeated results against a bad year | 3–5 harvests |
| **A generation** | Requires reorganising how people live and work, and the consent of those people | 20–50 years, even when trivially easy to copy |

**This factor usually dominates.** The most common error is to let legibility decide everything. A village cannot adopt three-field rotation because one man liked the look of it: the strips must be redivided, the fallow rotation agreed, and every household persuaded to risk a harvest. That is why genuinely simple agricultural improvements took generations to spread in reality.

### Putting them together

- **Any factor at its worst caps the whole.** Opaque means it does not spread without espionage, however valuable. Generation-scale proof means it crawls, however simple.
- **All three favourable** gives the 1-year and 5-year defaults.
- **When in doubt**, ask what a specific person would have to do tomorrow morning to copy it, and whether he would believe it was worth doing.

## Accelerators and brakes

### Accelerators

| What the player does | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Teaches it to an ally, or sells it openly | 1 year to general use (legible tech) |
| Uses it in a battle with allies present | 5 years |
| Lets monks copy a description of it | Very fast and very wide: a text at Ferrières or Auxerre reaches every scriptorium in the kingdom, and survives him |
| Trains outsiders, takes apprentices from other estates | 1–2 years, and the men carry it home permanently |
| Shows it to the king or at an assembly | Fast, and it becomes associated with the crown rather than with him |
| Uses it to make money visibly | Merchants are the fastest vector in the game; anything profitable and legible spreads along the trade roads |

### Brakes

| What the player does | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Keeps the work behind walls, with trusted men | Doubles or trebles the timeline for opaque processes |
| Uses it only against Vikings | No exposure: the witnesses do not survive or do not care |
| Keeps no written description | Slows transmission sharply and risks losing it on his death |
| Swears his craftsmen to secrecy, or keeps them dependent | Works until one is bought (see below) |
| Never uses it where a neighbour, churchman or royal officer can see | Can hold indefinitely, at the cost of never benefiting from it beyond his own lands |

### Espionage

The shortcut past every brake. A bribed or captured craftsman from the player's works cuts an opaque programme from decades to a few years, because he knows the shape of the process even if not every detail: where the white crystals come from, roughly what is mixed with what, what the beds smell like when they are ready.

- **Who tries:** a rival count, a bishop with money, the king, a great abbey. Anyone with resources and a reason.
- **When:** after any public demonstration of an opaque technology's results. Seeing a wall come down tells a man nothing about how, but it tells him it is worth any price to find out.
- **The player's defences:** pay his specialists well, keep them few, keep them loyal, keep them close. A craftsman with land of his own and a married daughter in the household does not leave.
- **Tie-in:** the king's escalation ladder already has a bought vassal. The same man, or another, can be bought to walk out with a process instead of to arrange an ambush.

## Worked examples

| Technology | Legibility | Reproduction | Proof | Time to spread, unaided |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Counterweight trebuchet | Obvious | Craftsman | Self-proving | **1–5 years.** Anyone who stands in front of one sees the box of rocks and the sling; carpenters do the rest. Expect it in Frankish hands within a year of its first public use, and in Viking hands within a decade |
| Stirrups, horse collar, nailed shoes | Obvious | Trivial to craftsman | Self-proving | **1–3 years** once seen in use |
| Crossbow improvements | Obvious | Craftsman | Self-proving | **2–5 years** |
| Three-field rotation | Visible but puzzling | Trivial | A generation | **20–50 years.** Trivially easy to copy and still slow: the strips must be redivided, the fallow agreed, every household persuaded to risk a harvest, and the lord must compel it. This is the classic case where ease of copying is irrelevant |
| Legumes in rotation, composting, marling | Visible but puzzling | Trivial | Several cycles | **10–30 years**, faster on estates with a single decisive lord |
| Drainage, ridge and furrow | Obvious | Capital | A season | **5–15 years**, limited by who can afford the labour |
| Watermill, improved gearing | Obvious | Capital | Self-proving | **3–10 years.** Mills are profitable and visible; lords copy anything that earns |
| Boiling water, clean sickrooms, isolating the sick | Visible but puzzling | Trivial | Several cycles | **15–40 years.** The practice is easy; the reasoning is unacceptable. Expect it to spread as a *custom* with a religious explanation attached, long before anyone accepts why it works |
| Sorting ergot from rye | Visible but puzzling | Trivial | A season | **5–15 years** once an outbreak makes the connection visible |
| Rations, field sanitation, boiled water on campaign | Obvious | Trivial | A season | **5–10 years.** Armies copy whatever wins; a contingent that does not sicken is noticed |
| Soap at scale, distillation | Opaque | Programme | Self-proving | **Does not spread unaided.** Needs espionage or teaching |
| Gunpowder | Opaque product, **visible process** | Programme | Self-proving, overwhelmingly | **15–25 years.** The beds cannot be hidden: everyone knows the lord has men collecting urine and dung and turning covered beds for a year. The product teaches nothing, but the proof is so overwhelming that a rival will fund a decade of failures on hints alone. Cut to **3–5 years** if a craftsman is bought or captured |
| Printing, movable type | Opaque | Programme | A generation | **Effectively never** in this period without direct teaching; the demand is not there |

### Judging something not on this list

Ask, in order:

1. **Could a competent craftsman reverse it from looking at it?** If no, it does not spread without espionage or teaching, whatever its value.
2. **What does copying it require that the copier does not already have?** Tools, a trade, money, or a process nobody knows how to start.
3. **Who has to be persuaded, and how many harvests or battles will that take?** If the answer involves persuading a village to change how it farms, assume a generation.
4. **Is the process visible even if the product is not?** If so, rivals know *something* is happening, which is enough to start a programme and enough to make espionage worth paying for.

## What diffusion means in play

### Once something has spread

| Affects | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| The player's advantage | Gone, or reduced to doing it better than others. A trebuchet is no edge when every siege has one |
| Allies | Stronger. Their contingents arrive with the player's equipment and methods |
| Enemies | Likewise. Anything that has spread may be used against him, including by Vikings and by the king |
| The kingdom | Richer, better fed, better defended. Famines bite less; sieges end faster; armies sicken less |
| Prices | A method that raises yields everywhere lowers grain prices everywhere, which cuts the player's own profit from his improved estate |

### The strategic choice

Secrecy and spread are a real trade-off, and neither is simply correct.

- **Hoard:** the player keeps his edge, wins his battles, stays ahead of rivals, and leaves weaker allies and a kingdom no better off than he found it.
- **Share:** allies and the kingdom grow strong, famines are survived, the Northmen are met with better walls and better engines, and the player must one day fight men he taught.

**The king quest makes this explicit.** At the final battle, everything the player gave away or demonstrated publicly is in the field on both sides. A player who spent eighty years improving Francia fights an enemy armed with his own ideas. That is the intended shape of the ending, not a flaw to design around.

### At the end of the game

The closing summary (see the Event Delivery Scripts doc) asks what the player introduced and what became of it. Track for each innovation:

- Whether it stayed on his lands, spread to the region, or reached the whole kingdom.
- Whether anyone wrote it down, which is what decides if it survives his death.
- Whether it is remembered as his.

A player who kept everything secret and left no text takes it all with him: the villa is prosperous, and in twenty years it is a villa like any other. A player who taught it, endowed a text at Auxerre or Ferrières, and let it spread changes the century, and may not be credited with any of it. Both are legitimate endings, and the summary should say plainly which one he chose.
