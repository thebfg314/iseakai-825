# Isekai 825: Historical Event Arcs, 825–910
Sep 24, 2026 · @Neil Jensen
## How to use this doc
Every event is written from one seat: a minor noble at the villa of Fontenay, in the pagus of Autun, northern Burgundy. Each arc lists what happened, how it reaches Fontenay (as news, as a summons, as soldiers on the road, or as smoke on the horizon), and how hard it is to change.
The neighborhood that matters most: Autun (your count and bishop), Chalon-sur-Saône and Mâcon to the east, Auxerre and Sens to the northwest, Langres and Dijon to the northeast, Nevers to the west. The Saône–Rhône valley is the highway south to Lyon, Provence and Italy; the Yonne and Seine lead north to Paris.
### Historical momentum (1–100)
Momentum is scored for a player at the start of the game: one minor noble with one villa. As the player gains land, allies and knowledge, the practical score of many events should drop.
| Score | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 90–100 | Structural. The player can prepare, profit or survive, but not prevent. | A solar eclipse; an emperor dying of old age |
| 70–89 | Driven by great powers. Years of player influence might bend details, not the outcome. | Who wins a civil war between kings |
| 40–69 | Contingent. Rests on a few people's choices; a well-placed noble could tip it. | A count switching sides before a battle |
| 10–39 | Fragile. Local, preventable with warning, organization or better methods. | A regional famine blunted by better farming and grain storage |
| 1–9 | Near-accident. Almost any interference changes it. | A single raid that succeeded only because no one kept watch |

Dates marked c. are approximate or disputed in the sources. Scores are design judgments, not historical facts: tune them freely.
## Rank tiers
The player climbs five tiers. Each tier changes how events reach them: from things that happen to them, to choices they make, to situations they own.
| Tier | Typical holding | Answers to | Role in events | Period examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Villa lord (start) | About 10 manses: one estate and its dependent farms | The count of Autun | Pays levies, answers the call-up, rides with a handful of men; local choices only | Unnamed vassals in charters |
| Viscount | Runs part or all of a pagus as the count's deputy; holds local courts | The count | Leads a local contingent; enforces or bends the count's orders; first taste of politics | Viscounts (vicecomites) become common later in the 9th century |
| Count | A pagus as a royal officer: courts, levies, tolls, defense | The king (in theory) | Chooses sides in civil wars; commands hundreds; courted by rival kings | Warin at Chalon (834) |
| Magnate | Several counties, with titles like marquis or duke | Nominally the king; in practice, nobody | Tips battles and successions; makes and unmakes kings | Bernard of Septimania, Warin at Fontenoy, Boso, Richard of Autun |
| King | A kingdom, by election and coronation | God and the great men who elected you | Historical events become the player's decisions | Odo (888); Boso (879) |

Two timing notes for the progression path. Charlemagne's rules expected a holder of about 12 manses to own a mail shirt, so a 10-manse lord starts just below the line of a fully armored warrior. And a non-Carolingian king is almost unthinkable before Boso in 879; after 888 it becomes normal. A player trying to become king earlier needs a Carolingian marriage or a truly exceptional reputation. In the game's rank system this tier is Duke (Latin dux, a title Bernard and Warin both carried); avoid "baron" in dialogue, since it's a later title. Renown thresholds: Minor Noble 1, Viscount 100, Count 500, Duke 1000, King 2000 (see the Event Delivery Scripts doc).
## The six crises
The arcs below collapse into six crises. The player only needs to know which crisis is active, what phase it's in, and which side they're on; individual events are beats inside a phase. Famines, sky events, church life and the economy are background systems that run underneath.
| Crisis | Years | Phases | Arcs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. The Succession Crisis | 829–844 | Rebellions (829–835) · Uneasy peace (835–840) · War of the Brothers (840–843) · Settlement and purges (843–844) | 1, 2 |
| 2. The Western Wars | 844–874 | Aquitaine resists (844–852) · Brittany wins (845–851) · Slow attrition (852–874) | 4, 5 |
| 3. Charles the Bald's Empire | 855–877 | Border shuffles (855–863) · Invasion and loyalty test (858–859) · Expansion (869–875) · Overreach and death (876–877) | 6 |
| 4. The Northmen | 841–911 | Distant raids (841–860) · River war and bridge forts (860–884) · Paris and the invasion of Burgundy (885–888) · Burgundy strikes back (888–911) | 3 |
| 5. The Collapse of the Kings | 877–898 | Weak heirs (877–884) · Boso's kingdom (879–882) · One emperor again, and his fall (884–888) · Odo vs Charles the Simple (888–898) | 7, 8 |
| 6. Richard's Burgundy | 880–911 | Richard takes Autun (880–882) · Building a principality (882–898) · Marquis and judge (898–911) | 7 |

Breathing spaces for building, farming and local stories: 825–829, 836–839, roughly 845–857 (at home, not in the west), and 899–910. The busiest stretches at home are 833–834, 840–844, 879–882 and 885–888.
## Keystones and dependencies
Track eight keystones, not every event. When the player changes a keystone, check only the events that depend on it, using this rule:
- Momentum 80+: still happens; adapt names and details (a famine still hits, a king still dies).
- Momentum 40–79: re-check its "requires" line; if it fails, use the fallback.
- Momentum under 40: drop it or replace it with a generic local event.

## Arc 1: The emperor and his sons (829–840)
Louis the Pious is overthrown by his own sons twice and comes back both times; he dies in 840 with the succession unsettled. The spark is his fourth son, Charles (the future Charles the Bald, born 823 to his second wife Judith), whose share of the empire has to come out of his elder brothers' inheritance.
This arc lands close to home. Bernard of Septimania, the emperor's chamberlain and the most hated man at court, is himself count of Autun, from about 826 until he is stripped of it in the 830 revolt. And the bloodiest local episode, the sack of Chalon-sur-Saône in 834, happens about 50 km east of Fontenay.

## Arc 2: War of the Brothers — Fontenoy and Verdun (840–844)
Lothair claims the whole empire; Charles the Bald and Louis the German ally against him and win at Fontenoy in June 841. The Treaty of Verdun (August 843) then splits the empire in three, and puts Autun in Charles's western kingdom, the ancestor of France.
For the player this is the pivotal arc. Fontenoy is fought near Auxerre, roughly 120 km from Fontenay (don't confuse the two names in dialogue). The battle turns when Count Warin arrives late with his southern and Burgundian troops, so a Burgundian noble can plausibly be the difference.

| 25 June 841 | Battle of Fontenoy. Lothair and Pippin II have the upper hand until Warin's army arrives. Charles and Louis win; losses are remembered as appalling on both sides. | 14 Feb 842 | Oaths of Strasbourg: Charles and Louis swear alliance before their armies, in early French and German. | Far off, but it is the first written text in a form of French; a cultural touchstone for a player from the future. | 90 |

Player hooks: pick a side in 840; bring troops, scouts or supplies to Fontenoy; hedge like Bernard did; care for the wounded with modern medicine and earn a reputation; win land when Autun changes hands in 844.
## Arc 3: Vikings on the rivers (841–911)
Viking raids grow from coastal hit-and-run to armies that winter inland, besiege Paris, and in 886–898 raid Burgundy itself. For 40 years they are distant news and refugees; from 886 they are a direct threat, arriving up the Yonne and Seine.

## Arc 4: Brittany breaks away (831–890)
Brittany goes from a Frankish-appointed governorship to an independent kingdom that beats Charles the Bald twice in the field. It is 500 km from Fontenay, so it matters as a drain on royal armies, a place nobles get sent to die, and proof the king can lose.

## Arc 5: Aquitaine and Pippin II (838–864)
The Aquitanians refuse Charles and back their own Carolingian, Pippin II, for 25 years. It pulls Burgundian troops west again and again, and Pippin ends up allied with Vikings.

## Arc 6: The reign of Charles the Bald (843–877)
Charles rules the west for 34 years: invaded by his brother, fighting Vikings and Bretons, grabbing half of Lotharingia, and finally crowned emperor in 875. Burgundy is his loyal heartland in several crises, and the border with the middle kingdom runs through the Saône valley next door.

## Arc 7: Boso, Richard, and the birth of Burgundy (877–910)
This is the player's home arc. Two brothers fight over the region: Boso makes himself a king in 879, and his younger brother Richard stays loyal to the Carolingians, takes Autun in 880, and spends 40 years building it into the Duchy of Burgundy. The player's direct lord changes, and so does what "lord" means.

## Arc 8: Paris besieged and the empire's last reunion (882–898)
The young kings die one after another, the West Frankish nobles invite the East Frankish emperor Charles the Fat to rule, and the empire is briefly whole again. His handling of the Siege of Paris, sending the Vikings into Burgundy, destroys his reputation. After his fall in 887 the empire splits for good, and Odo, the hero of Paris, becomes the first non-Carolingian king in the west.

## Arc 9: Saracens from the south, Magyars from the east

## Church, councils and scholars
This is the Carolingian Renaissance, and the player lives near several of its centers: Lyon, Auxerre, Ferrières, Fleury, and at the end, Cluny. Scholars are the people most able to understand, write down and spread whatever the player introduces. Churchmen also run half the land and most of the record-keeping.

## Famines, disease, weather and the sky
These are where modern knowledge pays off most directly. Famines come roughly every 10–25 years, usually from heavy rain and hard winters; better rotation, drainage, seed selection and grain storage can blunt them locally. Sky events can't be changed, but a player who predicts an eclipse or explains a comet gains enormous prestige, or suspicion.

## Coinage and economy
The economy runs on the silver penny (denier) and on land. Over the period, royal control of money peaks with the Edict of Pîtres in 864, which names Chalon-sur-Saône as one of only nine authorized mints, then slides toward local lords. Huge silver payments to Vikings pull coin out of circulation and into new taxes.
| Date | Event | At Fontenay | Momentum |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| c. 822–840 | Louis the Pious's standard denier (with a temple and the words CHRISTIANA RELIGIO) circulates empire-wide. | One trusted coin for trade. Game-start baseline. | 85 |
| 834–837 | Vikings raid the great trading port of Dorestad (Frisia) year after year. | Northern luxury goods get scarcer and dearer. | 80 |
| 845–884 | Tribute to Vikings: 7,000 pounds of silver in 845, and later payments in the thousands of pounds (c. 866, 877, 884). | Special levies on every estate, collected per manse. Coin gets scarce locally. | 70 |

Player hooks: get a post at the Chalon mint; standardize weights and measures on your land; bank silver before tribute levies; trade Burgundian wine and grain north when famine prices peak.
